Teotihuacan & Guadalupe Shrine: A Day Trip Into Mexico's Ancient Mysteries

By Harry

I'm going to be honest, standing at the base of the Pyramid of the Sun, looking up at this ancient structure that once dominated a city of 125,000 people, I felt genuinely small. Not in a bad way, but in that rare travel moment where you realize you're standing somewhere that fundamentally changes how you think about human history.

Hannah and I traveled to Mexico City for Día de los Muertos in 2024 (more on that another time because Día de los Muertos was amazing), but let's focus on this trip. We took a day trip to Teotihuacan, and it was one of the highlights of our Mexico trip. We're talking about pyramids built by a civilization so mysterious that even the Aztecs who discovered them centuries later had absolutely no idea who constructed them. And yes, these structures are every bit as impressive as you've seen in photos.

If you're planning a trip to Mexico City and thinking "should I do Teotihuacan?", the answer is absolutely yes. This wasn't just another tourist site. This was one of those experiences that stays with you long after you've left.

The Mystery No One Can Solve

Here's what makes Teotihuacan absolutely fascinating, and why our guide spent the first 20 minutes of the tour just setting up the mystery. When the Aztecs discovered this abandoned city around 1300 AD, it had already been deserted for over 600 years. They found these massive pyramids, a perfectly planned city laid out on a grid, and evidence of a civilization that at its peak (around 450 AD) was one of the largest cities in the entire world.

And they had absolutely no idea who built it. Think about that for a second. The Aztecs, who were building their own empire and constructing incredible temples, stumbled across Teotihuacan and were so bewildered by what they found that they decided only gods could have built it. They named it "Teotihuacan," which means "the place where the gods were created" in Nahuatl, and incorporated it into their mythology as the birthplace of the sun and moon.

Even today, with all our modern archaeology, carbon dating, and ground-penetrating radar, we still don't know who the Teotihuacanos were. We don't know what language they spoke. We don't know what they called their city. We don't even know why they abandoned this thriving metropolis around 550 AD, leaving behind one of the most impressive archaeological sites in the Americas.

Getting There: Our Tour from Mexico City

We booked our tour through GetYourGuide, which included transport from Mexico City and a guide for Teotihuacan. The tour picked us up early morning (around 7am) from a meeting point in Mexico City, and we were at Teotihuacan before the worst of the crowds arrived.

I'd absolutely recommend doing a guided tour rather than going independently. Our guide brought the site to life with stories, historical context, and explanations we would have completely missed wandering around on our own. Plus, you don't have to worry about navigating buses or taxis, which makes the whole day way less stressful.

The Pyramids: What You Need to Know About Climbing

When we visited in 2024, you could NOT climb the pyramids. Both the Pyramid of the Sun and the Pyramid of the Moon were closed to climbing for conservation purposes.

Update as of May 2025: The Pyramid of the Moon has partially reopened, but you can only climb the first section (the first 5 platforms, about 47 steps). The Pyramid of the Sun remains completely closed to climbing. So if you're planning a trip, check the current status before you go.

The Pyramid of the Sun

This is the big one. The Pyramid of the Sun is the third-largest pyramid in the world and the largest structure in Teotihuacan. It's absolutely massive, over 200 feet tall, with a base roughly the same size as the Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt. What's fascinating is that it was built in a single construction phase around 200 AD. They planned it, gathered materials, and constructed this enormous structure in one go.

The Pyramid of the Moon

The Pyramid of the Moon sits at the northern end of the Avenue of the Dead and is slightly smaller than the Pyramid of the Sun, but arguably more impressive because of its setting. It sits at the base of Cerro Gordo mountain, and the pyramid was deliberately designed to mirror the mountain's shape behind it.

Hot Air Balloon Rides: The One Thing We Didn't Do

One thing we didn't do but absolutely wish we had was the hot air balloon ride over Teotihuacan. You can book sunrise balloon flights that take you up over the pyramids and the entire archaeological site, and apparently, the views are absolutely spectacular. It costs around £100-150 per person, but seeing the sun rise over the valley would have been incredible. If we go back, this is 100% on the list.

The Vendors: Be Careful What You Buy

Outside the pyramids, there are loads of vendors selling souvenirs. Obsidian jewelry, masks, figurines, pottery. Some of it's genuine, and some of it's fake. We bought an obsidian plate, and our guide showed us a really useful trick for testing if obsidian is real.

How to Test Real Obsidian: Real obsidian is volcanic glass. If it's real, you can scratch the surface with a coin and it will leave a mark. Another test is weight—real obsidian is quite heavy for its size. And if you hold it up to the light, real obsidian should have some translucency around the edges.

The Food: Tamales You Can't Miss

After exploring the pyramids, we stopped at a place near the site that was making fresh tamales, and honestly, they were some of the best we had in Mexico. Tamales are a traditional Mexican dish made of masa (corn dough) filled with meats, cheeses, or vegetables, then wrapped in corn husks and steamed. They cost about 30-50 pesos each (roughly £1.20-2).

Practical Tips for Your Teotihuacan Visit

What to Bring: Comfortable walking shoes, sun protection (hat, sunscreen, sunglasses), water bottle, and cash for vendors.

Best Time to Visit: October to March is the best time weather-wise. Try to get there early (7-8am) to beat the crowds and the heat.

Altitude: Teotihuacan sits at about 2,300 meters (7,500 feet) above sea level. Take it slow, stay hydrated, and don't push yourself too hard.
